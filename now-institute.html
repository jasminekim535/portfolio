The client

What is the Now Institute?

The NOW Institute is the urban research wing of Morphosis Architects in Culver City, led by Thom Mayne and Eui-Sung Yi. It works with cities and communities, from Haiti to Madrid to Los Angeles, on planning and recovery strategies.

During my internship I designed two separate websites for the institute. “Now Institute” is the client; nowinstitute.org is one of the two sites.

01Noto Nownotonow.orgA new bilingual site for the institute’s research on rebuilding Japan’s Noto Peninsula after the 2024 earthquake. 02Now Institutenowinstitute.orgA redesign of the institute’s own website: its people, projects, publications and research.
Challenge

Outdated content, broken pages and two disconnected sites hid the institute’s work.

Solution

One rebuilt, responsive site for the institute, and a separate bilingual site for Noto Now.

Result

Two live sites with a simple shared design system, launched in three months.

Process: designing and vibe coding

I designed both sites in Figma, then built them myself by vibe coding: describing layouts and behavior to Claude Code in plain language, reviewing the code it wrote and refining it in the browser. Connected to Figma through MCP, Claude Code could read my designs directly.

  1. 01BenchmarkSimilar institutions, Figma Make
  2. 02DesignFigma layouts & design system
  3. 03BuildClaude Code + Figma MCP
  4. 04DeployGitHub & Vercel

Design in the real medium

Instead of handing off static mockups, I tested type, spacing and interactions as working pages, on real screens, and adjusted them there.

Faster loops with stakeholders

Every change could be deployed to Vercel and shared as a link, so feedback came from the live site rather than from screenshots.

From designer to builder

Owning the code, the GitHub history and the deploys meant I could ship two complete sites during a three-month internship.

Noto Now

A bilingual Japanese and English site for the Noto Peninsula recovery project, made with the City of Wajima and university partners. The narrative moves from the earthquake’s impact to the region’s culture and rebuilding vision.

Design thinking: tell it as a story

Most visitors arrive knowing little about Noto. If the site opened with master plans, the proposals would have no context. So I ordered every page as a story that builds understanding before asking the reader to judge a design.

  1. 01ImpactThe January 2024 earthquake, in numbers and images
  2. 02PlaceWhat is at stake: lacquerware, the Kiriko festival, food culture
  3. 03ResponseStrategies that rebuild around those strengths
  4. 04EvidenceSite images and the people behind the work

Zoom in, scale by scale

The Design page moves from four regional strategies for Ishikawa, to urban strategies for Wajima, to architectural speculations, so readers always know which scale they are looking at.

Bilingual by default

Every section pairs Japanese and English, so the same page works for local partners like the City of Wajima and for an international audience.

A tone that respects the subject

A serif type system, muted colors and large photography slow the pace down, so the story of a disaster reads with care rather than as a product pitch.

Now Institute

A redesign of the institute’s own website. Scattered projects, people and publications brought into one clear identity.

Research: getting to know every project

Much of the institute’s work had never been explained in one place. Before designing any page, I studied each architectural and urban project, publication and research theme: where it is, what problem it takes on, who it was made with and what came out of it.

Understand before laying out

Knowing each project let me write accurate summaries, choose the images that explain it best and connect related projects, publications and research.

One structure for every project

Comparing all thirteen showed what each project page needed to cover, so every page could follow the same clear structure.

Before → after

Design thinking: an archive you can browse

Over twenty years of work had piled up across disconnected pages. Instead of telling one story, this site needed to let different visitors find their own way in.

Organize by what people look for

Everything sits in five clear sections: About, People, Projects, Publications and Research, each with a consistent card layout.

Home page as a table of contents

The home page previews each section in turn, so a first-time visitor sees the full range of the institute’s work in one scroll.

Let the work lead

A restrained black-and-white system and a geometric sans-serif keep the focus on project imagery, and give the institute one recognizable identity.

Key takeaways

Working inside an architecture office, with real clients and partners, taught me as much about people as about interfaces.

01

Balance stakeholder needs

The institute’s leadership, partners such as the City of Wajima and the universities, and the public each wanted different things. I learned to weigh those needs and find designs that served all of them.

02

Present, listen, iterate

I regularly presented my work, took feedback from the team and turned it into concrete changes, explaining my decisions in plain language along the way.

03

Coordinate to get what you need

Much of the content lived with other people. I learned to delegate requests, reach the right person and follow up to collect the images, text and approvals the sites needed.

04

Research before design

Studying every project first made the content accurate and the structure obvious, and helped me talk with the architects about their work.

05

Narrative is part of the UX

The order of content shapes understanding: context before proposals for Noto Now, an archive to browse for the institute.

06

Vibe coding makes designers builders

Building with Claude Code and the Figma MCP let me test ideas in the real medium, iterate quickly and ship two live sites myself.
